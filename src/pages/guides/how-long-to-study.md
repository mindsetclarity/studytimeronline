Ask ten students how long they study in one sitting and you'll hear everything from 20 minutes to four hours. Online advice isn't much help either: one source swears by 25 minutes, another says 52, a third says 90.

The honest answer is that **no single length suits everyone**. The right length depends on three things you can actually observe: the kind of task, how long you can currently stay focused, and how close your deadline is.

## Start from what you can do now

Session length is a skill you build, much like running distance. If you usually last 15 minutes before picking up your phone, a 90-minute block will fail, and failing makes the next session harder to start.

Try this for a few days:

1. Start a timer when you sit down to work.
2. Stop it the first time you notice you've drifted (you check your phone, reread a line three times, or open a new tab).
3. Write the number down.

The median of those numbers is your **current focus span**. Set your sessions a little above it: about 5 minutes longer if it's under 30 minutes, about 10 minutes longer if it's above. Raise the target only once you hit it comfortably most days.

## Match the length to the task

Some work takes a while to get into. Some work wears you out fast.

**Short blocks (15–25 minutes)** suit:
- Flashcards and vocabulary
- Quick formula or definition review
- Any task you've been putting off, because the short block lowers the barrier to starting

**Medium blocks (30–50 minutes)** suit:
- Reading a textbook chapter and taking notes
- Most problem sets
- Summarising lecture notes from memory

**Long blocks (60–90 minutes)** suit:
- Essay drafting, where you need the whole argument in your head
- Programming and long derivations
- Full mock exams, because the exam itself is long

You may have read that the brain runs on fixed 90-minute "ultradian" cycles. Those cycles have been described in sleep research, but the evidence that they set a perfect study length while you're awake is weak. Treat 90 minutes as an upper limit for very absorbing work, not a target.

## Breaks scale with the block

A useful rule of thumb is a break of about **one-fifth of the session length**:

| Session | Break |
| --- | --- |
| 25 min | 5 min |
| 45 min | 8–10 min |
| 60 min | 12 min |
| 90 min | 15–20 min |

After two to three hours of total focused time, take a real break of 30 minutes or more: a meal, a walk, or exercise. Long stretches without a proper break are where mistakes creep in, especially on maths and problem-solving tasks.

## How total daily hours fit in

The length of one session matters less than **how many good sessions you get through in a day**. Most students can do somewhere between three and six hours of deep, focused study per day. Past that, the extra hours are mostly rereading and copying notes, which feel productive but teach you little.

If the exam is far away, a steady routine of two to four hours a day, spread across the week, beats occasional long cramming days. If it's close, you can add hours, but keep the breaks. Cutting sleep or breaks to squeeze in more time usually costs more than it gains.

## Signs your session length is wrong

**Too long:**
- You check the clock often in the last third of the block
- Your notes in the second half are noticeably worse than in the first
- You dread starting the next block

**Too short:**
- The timer rings while you're in the middle of a thought
- You spend the first five minutes of every block just reloading context
- You often ignore the timer and keep going

Adjust by 5–10 minutes at a time, and track what you change so you can see what helps.

## A simple weekly experiment

If you're not sure, spend a week testing:

- **Monday–Tuesday:** 25/5 blocks
- **Wednesday–Thursday:** 45/10 blocks
- **Friday:** whichever felt better, used on your hardest subject

At the end of each day, write down how many blocks you finished and rate the quality of your work from 1 to 5. A week of honest notes will tell you more than any general rule.
