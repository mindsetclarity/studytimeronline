The Pomodoro Technique is a way of working in short, timed blocks with a break after each one. Francesco Cirillo came up with it as a university student in the late 1980s, timing himself with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. *Pomodoro* is Italian for tomato, and the name stuck.

The method is simple, and that's the point. It doesn't make you smarter or the material easier. It makes **starting** easier and stops a long session from slowly turning into an afternoon of half-work.

## The basic cycle

1. **Pick one task.** "Chapter 4 practice questions" counts as a task. "Study biology" doesn't.
2. **Set a timer for 25 minutes** and work only on that task until it rings.
3. **Take a 5-minute break.** Stand up, get water, look at something far away. Don't start a new task.
4. **After four Pomodoros, take a longer break** of 15 to 30 minutes.

That's the whole method. The rest of this guide covers the details that decide whether it works for you.

## Why a timer helps at all

Most procrastination comes from the size of the task, not laziness. "Revise for the exam" is vague and huge, so your brain treats it as a threat and looks for something easier. "Work on past-paper question 3 for 25 minutes" is small and has an end, and that makes it much easier to begin.

The timer also takes a decision off your plate. Without one, you keep asking yourself "should I stop now?", and every time you ask, you break your focus. With a timer, the answer is always "not until it rings".

Finally, the breaks are scheduled, so you don't need to earn them by feeling tired. Students who skip breaks tend to drift in the second hour: they're still at the desk, but they're rereading the same paragraph.

## The rules that matter most

**One task per Pomodoro.** If you finish early, use the leftover minutes to review what you just did. Don't start something new, because the next block should begin with a clear goal.

**Protect the block.** If a thought pops into your head ("I need to email my tutor"), write it on a scrap of paper and keep going. A list of interruptions you didn't act on is a good sign.

**Actually take the break.** Scrolling a phone isn't a break. It's a different kind of attention load, and it makes it harder to go back to the work. Move your body and rest your eyes instead.

**Count your Pomodoros.** After a week you'll know that a chapter of reading takes you about three blocks and a problem set about five. That makes planning far more accurate than guessing in hours.

## Adjusting the rhythm to the work

Twenty-five minutes is a starting point, not a rule. Different work suits different lengths:

| Kind of work | Suggested focus / break | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting a task you've been avoiding | 15 / 3 | The barrier to starting is the problem, so make the first block tiny. |
| Flashcards, vocabulary, formula drills | 25 / 5 | Repetitive work gets dull quickly, so short blocks keep it fresh. |
| Reading and note-taking | 30–40 / 5–8 | You need time to build up context on a chapter. |
| Long problem sets, essays, coding | 45–50 / 10 | Breaking off mid-problem costs a lot, so longer blocks pay off. |
| Mock exams | Match the real exam | Practise the conditions you'll face (see our [timed practice exam guide](/guides/timed-practice-exams/)). |

If you regularly hit the end of a block in the middle of a thought, your blocks are probably too short for that kind of work. If you're checking the clock with 10 minutes still to go, they're too long.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating every block as a race.** The aim is steady attention, not the most pages per minute.
- **Planning more blocks than you can do.** Eight good Pomodoros is a very strong study day for most students. Sixteen planned and six done feels like failing even though it isn't.
- **Skipping the long break.** After about two hours of focused work, your attention drops off. The long break is when you recover.
- **Using the technique for everything.** Lectures, group study and labs already have their own structure. Use Pomodoros for solo, self-directed work.

## A sample two-hour session

Here's what a realistic evening of revision for a chemistry test might look like:

1. **Pomodoro 1 (25 min):** Write down everything you remember about the topic from memory, then check it against your notes.
2. **Break (5 min)**
3. **Pomodoro 2 (25 min):** Work through the questions you got wrong last week.
4. **Break (5 min)**
5. **Pomodoro 3 (25 min):** Do new practice questions without your notes.
6. **Break (5 min)**
7. **Pomodoro 4 (25 min):** Mark your answers, then write a one-paragraph summary of what you still find confusing.
8. **Long break (20 min)**

Notice that almost every block asks you to *produce* something: an answer, a list, a summary. Rereading is the least effective way to use a Pomodoro. Our guide to [active recall and spaced repetition](/guides/active-recall-and-spaced-repetition/) explains why.
