Most study plans fail by lunchtime. It's rarely laziness. The plan was written for an imaginary person with unlimited energy and no interruptions. A good plan is one you finish on an ordinary day.

## Plan in blocks, not hours

"Study chemistry 9–12" doesn't tell you what to do at 9:01. Instead, break the day into **focus blocks**: fixed periods of single-task work, usually 25–50 minutes, each followed by a short break. (If you're unsure how long yours should be, see [how long a study session should be](/guides/how-long-to-study).)

Then give each block **one specific output**:

- ❌ "Chemistry"
- ✅ "Chemistry: redo the 8 equilibrium questions I got wrong on Tuesday"

A specific output tells you what to do first and how to know you're finished.

## Know your real capacity

Count how many focus blocks you actually completed on a few recent study days. That number, not your hopes, is your capacity. For many students it's 6 to 10 blocks of 25 minutes, or 4 to 6 blocks of 50 minutes.

Plan for **about 80% of your capacity**. The rest is buffer for things that take longer than expected, which they always do. A plan you finish builds momentum. A plan you abandon at 3 p.m. makes tomorrow's plan easier to ignore.

## Put hard work where your energy is

Most people have a period of the day when focus comes easily, often mid-morning, though everyone differs. Track a few days and you'll find yours.

- **Peak energy:** the hardest task of the day. New material, difficult problems, essay drafting.
- **Medium energy:** practice questions on familiar topics, reviewing for [active recall](/guides/active-recall-and-spaced-repetition).
- **Low energy:** admin. Organising notes, making flashcards, planning tomorrow.

The classic mistake is spending your best hours on easy tasks "to warm up" and leaving the hard work for the evening, when you're tired.

## A sample day

Here's a realistic plan for a student revising three subjects, with a capacity of about eight 25-minute blocks:

| Time | Block | Task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9:00 | 1 | Maths: 4 new integration problems from chapter 6 |
| 9:30 | 2 | Maths: 4 more, then check all 8 against the answers |
| 10:00 | — | Long break (20 min) |
| 10:20 | 3 | History: blank-page recall of the causes of WW1, then correct |
| 10:50 | 4 | History: plan one essay answer under timed conditions |
| 11:20 | — | Lunch and a walk |
| 13:00 | 5 | Biology: flashcards due today |
| 13:30 | 6 | Biology: past-paper questions on enzymes |
| 14:00 | — | Buffer / catch-up |
| 14:30 | 7 | Maths: mark the morning's work and redo any errors |
| 15:00 | 8 | Plan tomorrow, make flashcards from today's mistakes |

There's a whole slot left empty as buffer, and the last block is light. That's deliberate.

## Mix subjects, but not too much

Switching subjects between blocks helps you stay fresh, and mixing problem types within a subject (called interleaving) helps you learn to pick the right method in the exam. But switching every single block means you keep reloading context. Two blocks on the same subject before switching is a good default.

## The five-minute evening review

At the end of each day, spend five minutes answering three questions:

1. **What did I finish?** Tick it off. Notice the progress you made.
2. **What didn't I finish, and why?** Was the task too big, did you start late, or was it just a bad day? Each has a different fix.
3. **What are tomorrow's first two blocks?** Write them down specifically, so tomorrow starts without a decision.

That third question matters most. When the first task is already decided, getting started in the morning is much easier.

## When the day goes wrong

Some days will fall apart: you get ill, there's a family emergency, or you just can't focus. On those days, have a **minimum plan**: one or two blocks on your most important subject, and nothing else. Doing the minimum keeps the habit alive, and a streak that survives bad days is worth more than a perfect week followed by a week off.
