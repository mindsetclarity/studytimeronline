If you could change only one thing about how you study, the research points clearly in one direction: **spend less time rereading and more time testing yourself, spread out over days.**

These two ideas, active recall and spaced repetition, are among the best-supported findings in learning science. A large 2013 review by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated ten common study techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice were the only two rated "high utility". Highlighting and rereading, the methods most students rely on, were rated "low utility".

## Active recall: pull it out, don't put it in

Active recall means trying to bring information back from memory *without looking*. You might answer a question, explain a concept out loud, or write down everything you remember about a topic.

It works because retrieving a memory strengthens it. A well-known 2006 study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students either reread a passage or take recall tests on it. A week later, the students who had tested themselves remembered much more, even though the rereaders had seen the text more times and felt more confident.

That last point matters. **Rereading feels productive because the material looks familiar**, but recognising something isn't the same as being able to recall it in an exam.

### Ways to practise active recall

- **The blank page.** Close your notes and write everything you know about the topic. Then open your notes and fill the gaps in a different colour.
- **Questions instead of notes.** When you take notes, write them as questions ("What are the three stages of mitosis before cytokinesis?") and cover the answers.
- **Flashcards**, as long as you say the answer *before* you flip the card.
- **Teach it.** Explain the concept out loud to an imaginary student. Wherever you stall is where your understanding has a gap.
- **Past-paper questions** without notes, then mark them strictly.

Recall is supposed to feel hard. That effort is what makes the memory stronger. If every card is easy, either you've learned them or the questions are too easy.

## Spaced repetition: review just before you forget

Memory fades in a fairly predictable way. Hermann Ebbinghaus measured this in the 1880s and described what's now called the forgetting curve. Each time you successfully recall something, it fades more slowly afterwards. So reviews should start close together and spread further apart.

A large 2006 review of spacing research by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues found that spreading study across sessions consistently beats massing it into one sitting for the same total time. In other words, three 20-minute reviews across a week beat one hour the night before.

### A simple review schedule

You don't need software to start. After you first learn a topic, review it on roughly this schedule:

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Same day or next day |
| 2nd | 3 days later |
| 3rd | 1 week later |
| 4th | 2–3 weeks later |
| 5th | About a month later |

If you fail a recall badly, reset that topic to the start of the schedule. If it feels effortless, you can stretch the next gap.

The easiest way to track this is a notebook page per week, listing which topics are due each day. Spaced-repetition flashcard apps can automate it if you prefer.

## Putting it together with a timer

Active recall and timed blocks fit together well, because a timer gives recall a clear start and end. Here's a 50-minute block that uses both:

1. **10 minutes – blank page.** Write everything you remember about today's due topic.
2. **10 minutes – check and correct.** Compare with your notes and mark what you missed.
3. **20 minutes – questions.** Do practice questions focused on what you missed.
4. **10 minutes – new material.** Learn the next small section and turn it into 5–10 recall questions for tomorrow.

Run one or two of these blocks per subject per day and your reviews stay spaced without much planning.

## Common objections

**"It's slower than reading through my notes."** Yes, per page. But it gets material into long-term memory, so you spend less time relearning it later.

**"I keep getting things wrong."** Good. Getting things wrong and then correcting them is part of how this works. Getting things wrong in a practice session is far cheaper than getting them wrong in the exam.

**"I don't have time to space things out, the exam is next week."** You can still space within a week: review each topic on day 1, day 3 and day 6. That's still better than cramming all of it on day 6.
