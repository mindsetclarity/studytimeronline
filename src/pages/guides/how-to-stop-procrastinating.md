If you procrastinate on studying, you're probably not lazy. Most people who put off revision care about the result, often a lot. The problem is that the task feels unpleasant in some way: too big, too vague, too boring, or tied up with worry about doing badly. Putting it off makes that feeling go away for now, which is why it's such a hard habit to break.

The good news is that you don't need to feel motivated to start. You need to make starting easy enough that you do it anyway. Here's how.

## Work out what kind of "unpleasant" it is

Before trying to fix procrastination, notice which feeling is behind it. Different causes need different fixes:

- **Too big:** "Revise biology" has no edges. Your brain doesn't know where to begin, so it doesn't.
- **Too vague:** You know you should study, but not what exactly to do in the next ten minutes.
- **Too boring:** The task is clear but dull, like copying out formulas or vocabulary.
- **Too worrying:** Opening the material reminds you how much you don't know, or how close the exam is.

Most real cases are a mix. The steps below tackle all four.

## Shrink the task until it's obvious

Take whatever you're avoiding and keep breaking it down until the first step is something you could start in the next minute without thinking.

"Revise biology" → "Revise cell biology" → "Make flashcards for section 2.1" → "Write flashcards for the first five terms in section 2.1".

The last version is so small it feels almost silly. That's the point. Small tasks don't set off the same urge to escape, and once you've started, carrying on is much easier than starting was.

## Use a two-minute start

Tell yourself you'll work on the task for just two minutes, and that you're allowed to stop after that. Most of the time you won't stop, because the hardest part was the beginning. On the days you do stop, you've still done two minutes more than nothing, and you've practised starting.

## Put it on a timer

A timer turns an open-ended task into a bounded one. "Work on this until I've finished" can feel endless. "Work on this for 25 minutes" has a clear finish line.

Open the [Pomodoro timer](/pomodoro/) and commit to one round. You don't have to commit to the whole afternoon, just one round. When it rings, take the break, then decide whether to do another. Most people do. See [the Pomodoro Technique guide](/guides/pomodoro-technique/) for how to adjust the lengths.

## Lower the bar for the first attempt

Perfectionism is a big driver of procrastination. If you feel the first attempt has to be good, starting feels risky. Give yourself permission to do it badly:

- Write a deliberately rough first draft of the essay paragraph.
- Try the practice question even if you think you'll get it wrong, then check the answer.
- Read the chapter once quickly without taking notes.

A rough first attempt gives you something to improve, which is much easier than facing a blank page.

## Remove the easy escapes

When the task feels uncomfortable, you'll reach for whatever is easiest, and that's usually your phone. Put it in another room before you start. If you study on a laptop, close every tab except the one you need. See [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/phone-distraction-while-studying/) for more ways to add friction.

## Decide the night before

Mornings are full of small decisions, and each one is a chance to put things off. The evening before, write down the first task for tomorrow and when you'll start it: "9:00, maths questions 1–10". When the time comes, the decision has already been made. The [study planner](/study-planner/) is handy for this: add tomorrow's first task before you finish today.

## When it's worry, not laziness

If the task feels worrying, for example because the exam is close and you feel behind, avoiding it makes the worry grow. A few things help:

- **Find out where you really stand.** Do a handful of past-paper questions. The reality is usually less bad than the vague dread.
- **Make a plan.** A [revision timetable](/guides/revision-timetable/) turns "I'm behind on everything" into a list of specific things to do this week.
- **Talk to someone.** A teacher, tutor or friend can often help you see what to focus on first.

## Be kind to yourself afterwards

Studies of students have found that those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam tended to procrastinate less before the next. Beating yourself up adds to the bad feeling around the task, which makes you more likely to avoid it again. Notice it, make the next step smaller, and start again.

## A quick checklist

1. Name the feeling: too big, too vague, too boring or too worrying?
2. Shrink the task until the first step takes under a minute to start.
3. Phone in another room.
4. Start a 25-minute timer and commit to one round only.
5. Allow the first attempt to be rough.
6. Before you finish, write down tomorrow's first task.
