Knowing the material and performing under time pressure are different skills. Many students who "knew it all" run out of time in the real exam, not because they're slow, but because they've never practised pacing.

A timed practice exam at home trains that second skill. Done properly, it's one of the most useful hours you can spend in the weeks before an exam.

## Step 1: Choose the right paper

Use a real past paper or an official sample paper whenever you can. Their difficulty, wording and mark distribution are much closer to the real thing than questions you write yourself. If you've used up the past papers, mix questions from different years into a new paper of the same length.

Save at least one full paper you haven't seen for the final week. Sitting a paper you've already practised tests your memory, not your exam technique.

## Step 2: Set up real conditions

The closer your practice is to the exam room, the more useful it is:

- **Same length.** If the exam is 2 hours 15 minutes, set the timer for exactly that.
- **Same time of day**, if you can. A morning exam practised at midnight isn't the same test.
- **Same materials.** Only the calculator, formula sheet and paper you'll be allowed.
- **Phone in another room**, not face-down on the desk.
- **No pausing.** If you'd keep going in the exam, keep going here.

Tell anyone you live with that you're sitting a mock exam, so they don't interrupt you.

## Step 3: Make a time budget

Before you start, work out how many minutes each mark is worth:

> **minutes per mark = (total time − reading and checking time) ÷ total marks**

For example, take a 90-minute paper worth 80 marks, and keep 10 minutes for reading at the start and checking at the end:

> (90 − 10) ÷ 80 = **1 minute per mark**

So a 6-mark question should take about 6 minutes, and a 15-mark essay about 15. Write the target finishing time for each section in the margin before you start. For a three-section paper starting at 2:00, that might be: section A by 2:25, section B by 2:55, section C by 3:20, then checking until 3:30.

## Step 4: Pace during the exam

- **Check the clock at your milestones**, not constantly. Glancing at the timer every minute adds stress and saves nothing.
- **Skip and come back.** If a question is taking 50% longer than its budget, mark it and move on. A blank 3-mark question costs less than an unfinished 15-mark one.
- **Answer the easy marks first** in each section, if the exam lets you choose the order.
- **Keep the checking time.** Students who skip checking routinely lose marks on arithmetic and on questions they misread.

A large, calm countdown helps here. You want to see the time remaining at a glance, without doing sums in your head.

## Step 5: Review properly

Marking the paper is only half the job. Sort every lost mark into one of these groups:

| Cause | What it looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Didn't know it** | You couldn't have answered even with unlimited time | Back to the material: flashcards, notes, practice questions |
| **Misread the question** | You answered a different question from the one asked | Underline command words ("explain", "compare", "calculate") |
| **Ran out of time** | You knew it but didn't reach it | Stick to the time budget more strictly; practise skipping |
| **Careless error** | Arithmetic, units, copying mistakes | Use your checking time; slow down on the final step |

Count the marks in each group. If most of your losses are timing or careless errors, more revision won't help much. More timed practice will.

## How often to sit mock exams

- **Six or more weeks out:** sit timed *sections* (one question type, 20–30 minutes) once or twice a week, alongside normal revision.
- **Two to six weeks out:** one full paper per subject per week.
- **Final two weeks:** one or two full papers per subject, with a full review after each. Don't sit so many that you have no time left to fix what they reveal.

## Managing nerves

Timed practice also gets you used to the feeling of time pressure, which makes exam-day nerves less of a shock. If you notice panic rising during a mock, try this: put the pen down, breathe out slowly for a count of five, read the question again, and write the first line of an answer, even a rough one. Getting your pen moving usually breaks the freeze.
