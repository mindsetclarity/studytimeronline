You sit down to study, open your notes, and reach for your phone "just to check one thing". Forty minutes later you've watched a stream of reels and haven't read a page. It isn't a character flaw. Short-video feeds are built so that there is never a natural place to stop: each clip is short, the next one starts on its own, and every so often one is genuinely funny or useful, which keeps you scrolling for the next.

Trying harder rarely fixes this. Changing your setup does. This guide covers the changes that make the biggest difference, roughly in order of how much they help.

## 1. Put distance between you and the phone

The most effective step is also the least exciting: put the phone in another room while you study. Face-down on the desk isn't enough. Even a silent phone within reach pulls at your attention, because part of your mind keeps track of whether to check it.

If you need the phone nearby (for an authenticator code, or because someone may need to reach you in an emergency), put it behind you, out of sight, with only calls from favourite contacts allowed through.

## 2. Add friction to the apps that pull you in

Every extra step between you and the feed is a moment to change your mind. Ways to add friction, from mild to strong:

- **Log out** of the apps you scroll most, so opening them means typing a password.
- **Move them off your home screen** into a folder on the last page.
- **Turn on grayscale** during study hours. Feeds are much less appealing in black and white.
- **Delete the app** during exam season and use the website, which is usually clumsier on purpose.

You don't need all of these. Pick the one you would actually keep doing.

## 3. Schedule your checks instead of resisting them

"Never check your phone" fails because it's not realistic. Messages do need answering. A better rule is "not during a timed block". Work in focus blocks with a [study timer](/timer/) or the [Pomodoro timer](/pomodoro/), and allow yourself to check messages at the start of a longer break.

This works because the urge to check is easier to postpone than to deny. "In 18 minutes" is a much easier answer than "no".

There's one exception. Short breaks of five minutes shouldn't be spent on a feed. Five minutes of scrolling rarely stays five minutes, and it makes the next block harder to start. Use short breaks to stand up, drink water and rest your eyes. Save the phone for the long break after a few rounds. Our guide to [study breaks](/guides/study-breaks/) has more ideas.

## 4. Set a daily limit you can see

Most people underestimate how much they scroll, sometimes by a lot. Before you try to cut back, measure. Your phone's built-in screen-time report shows how many minutes went to each app. Look at it for a normal week without changing anything.

Then set a limit that is lower than your average but not dramatically so, and make it visible. A number you see as you go ("12 of 30 reels today") changes behaviour more than a report you read at the end of the week. Built-in app limits on Android and iOS work for this, as do apps that count reels for you and remind you of your plan when you reach your limit.

## 5. Make the first five minutes of studying easy

A lot of phone-checking happens at the start of a session, when the work feels big and vague. The phone offers an easy escape from that feeling.

Before you sit down, decide on one small, specific first task: "redo question 4" or "summarise page 12 in five lines". Write it on paper next to you. When the first step is obvious, there's less of a gap for the phone to fill. Our guide on [how to stop procrastinating](/guides/how-to-stop-procrastinating/) goes further into this.

## 6. Notice what you were avoiding

When you catch yourself scrolling, ask what you were doing just before you picked up the phone. Often it's a specific moment: a hard question, a page you didn't understand, an essay paragraph that wasn't working. That's useful information. Note it down, then go back and make that one thing smaller. Ask for help, skip to an easier question and come back, or write a deliberately rough version.

## A simple setup to try this week

1. Phone in another room during timed blocks.
2. Feed apps logged out, or moved off the home screen.
3. Work in 25- to 45-minute blocks with a timer.
4. Check messages only at the start of a long break.
5. Look at your screen-time report on Sunday and compare it with last week.

Try it for a week before judging it. The first two or three days usually feel the hardest, because the habit of reaching for the phone is still there even when the phone isn't.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is listening to music on my phone OK?** Yes, if you start a playlist before the block and don't touch the phone until it ends. Choosing songs mid-session is where the scrolling sneaks in.

**What if I need my phone for studying?** Use a laptop or a printed copy if you can. If not, put the phone in focus or do-not-disturb mode, log out of the feed apps and keep only the study app open.

**Will I miss something important?** Very rarely in a 25- to 45-minute block. If you're worried, let calls from family or close contacts through and silence everything else.
