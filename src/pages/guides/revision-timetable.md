Most revision timetables fail in the first week. They're usually too ambitious, too evenly spread across subjects, and they leave no room for the days that go wrong. This guide shows you how to build one that survives contact with real life.

You'll need your exam dates, a list of topics for each subject, and about an hour.

## Step 1: Count the hours you actually have

Start with a calendar from today to your first exam. For each day, write down how many hours you could realistically study, not how many you wish you could. Account for school or lectures, work, travel, meals, sleep and the things you're not going to give up.

Then multiply by about two-thirds. Three hours at a desk is usually around two hours of focused work once breaks, setup and the odd distraction are counted. This number, your **real focus hours**, is what you plan with.

If the total looks small, that's useful to know now rather than the week before the exam.

## Step 2: List topics and rate them

For each subject, list the topics from the syllabus or course outline. Rate each one:

- **Red:** you don't understand it or can't answer questions on it.
- **Amber:** you understand it but make mistakes or are slow.
- **Green:** you can answer exam questions on it reliably.

Be honest. The quickest way to check is to try a few past-paper questions on each topic rather than going by how familiar the notes look. Familiar isn't the same as known.

## Step 3: Share the hours by need, not equally

An even split ("two hours of each subject every week") feels fair, but it wastes time on topics you already know. Instead, give most of your hours to red and amber topics, and to subjects that are worth more or come first.

A simple way to do it: give each red topic three units of time, each amber topic two, and each green topic one. Add up the units and divide your real focus hours between them.

## Step 4: Mix subjects within each day

Studying one subject for a whole day feels productive, but switching between two or three subjects in a day usually leads to better long-term memory, and it's less tiring. A good day might be one long block on a red topic, one on an amber topic from another subject, and a shorter review block at the end.

Put the hardest task first, while your attention is fresh.

## Step 5: Plan review, not just first passes

Learning a topic once isn't enough. You'll forget much of it within days unless you come back to it. Build short review sessions into the timetable: revisit each topic roughly one day, one week and three weeks after you first study it. Use those sessions to test yourself, not to reread. Our guide to [active recall and spaced repetition](/guides/active-recall-and-spaced-repetition/) explains how.

## Step 6: Add practice exams

In the last few weeks, past papers matter more than notes. Schedule at least one full, timed practice paper per subject, and leave time afterwards to review your mistakes. [How to run a timed practice exam](/guides/timed-practice-exams/) walks through the process.

## Step 7: Leave catch-up time

Every week, leave one half-day or two evenings empty. When a day goes wrong because you were ill, a topic took longer, or something came up, you move the missed work into the catch-up slot instead of letting the whole plan slide. A timetable with no slack breaks the first time life happens.

## What a week might look like

| Day | Block 1 (hardest) | Block 2 | Block 3 (review) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Maths: integration (red) | Chemistry: rates (amber) | Biology flashcards |
| Tue | Physics: circuits (red) | Maths: vectors (amber) | Review Monday's topics |
| Wed | Chemistry: equilibrium (red) | Biology: genetics (amber) | Maths flashcards |
| Thu | Maths: past paper, timed | Review paper mistakes | — |
| Fri | Biology: respiration (red) | Physics: waves (amber) | Review Wednesday's topics |
| Sat | Catch-up | Catch-up | — |
| Sun | Rest | — | — |

## Make each day's plan concrete

A timetable says what to study this week. Each morning, turn that day's slots into specific tasks with a time estimate: "15 integration questions from the textbook, 45 minutes". Then work through them with a timer. The [study planner](/study-planner/) is built for this: add the day's tasks and start each one straight into a timed block.

## Review the timetable every week

Once a week, check your topic ratings again. Move topics that have turned green down the list, give more time to anything still red, and adjust your hours if you consistently plan more than you do. The aim isn't to follow the first version perfectly. It's to keep a plan that still matches reality.
